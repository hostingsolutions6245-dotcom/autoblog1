Embedded finance is the integration of financial tools—such as payments, lending, insurance, or bank accounts—directly into nonfinancial software platforms, websites, or mobile applications. It enables users to access financial products at their exact point of need without relying on separate banking portals or third-party institutions.

Key takeaways
  • Embedded finance places financial functions directly into everyday apps, e-commerce stores, and software platforms.
  • Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) and regulated sponsor banks power these experiences behind the scenes.
  • Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) platforms originated nearly $160 billion in consumer credit in 2025 alone.
  • The market for embedded banking services is projected to expand from $30.3 billion in 2026 to $223 billion by 2036.

What is embedded finance and how does it work behind the scenes?

In traditional commerce, buying a product, obtaining financing, or getting insurance required interacting with separate institutions. Embedded finance removes these barriers by building financial capabilities into platforms consumers already use daily—like ride-hailing software, online marketplaces, or retail storefronts.

As noted in official remarks published on the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission website, technology fundamentally alters how finance is delivered, allowing financial incumbents, startups, and tech platforms to reshape core business models.

The core mechanism that makes this integration possible is the Application Programming Interface (API). APIs allow nonfinancial platforms to communicate directly with backend financial infrastructure. Here is how the process operates behind the scenes:

  • The Frontend Platform: A customer interacts with an app or site, such as ordering food, requesting a ride, or shopping online.
  • API Connectivity: When the customer initiates a financial action (like saving a card or selecting a payment plan), APIs instantly transmit secure data between the platform and financial providers.
  • Banking as a Service (BaaS) & Sponsor Banks: Regulated sponsor banks perform necessary ledger management, identity verification (KYC), fraud detection, and funds settlement in the background.
  • Execution: The transaction settles immediately inside the native interface, eliminating the need for external redirects.

By connecting nonfinancial user interfaces directly to backend ledger systems, companies offering fintech services can create friction-free customer workflows while sharing in financial service margins.

What are the main types of embedded finance products?

Embedded finance spans several distinct categories, ranging from daily micro-transactions to specialized lending programs and merchant accounts.

1. Embedded Payments

Embedded payments allow users to complete purchases within an application using stored credentials or single-tap checkouts. Examples include paying for a food delivery order directly inside an app, using a store app at a coffee counter, or utilizing automated ride-share billings. Instant payouts—where gig workers receive earnings immediately after a shift—also rely on embedded payment technology.

2. Embedded Lending and Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL)

Point-of-sale financing allows consumers to split transactions into short-term or long-term installment loans. According to research published by the Federal Reserve Board, BNPL providers originated close to $160 billion in consumer credit products in 2025. Standard "pay in 4" plans accounted for approximately half of that issuance, while other short- and longer-term installment loans made up the remainder. Notably, over 60 percent of total BNPL issuance carried a 0% APR.

3. Embedded Banking Accounts

Nonbank software platforms increasingly issue branded commercial accounts and debit cards to their users. For instance, e-commerce platforms offer business accounts directly to sellers, while gig economy services provide custom debit cards to drivers for instant access to pay and fuel rewards. According to Stripe's guide to embedded banking, revenue in the embedded banking market is projected to grow from $30.3 billion in 2026 to $223 billion by 2036, with the broader embedded finance sector reaching $1.7 trillion by 2034. Companies evaluating their checkout architecture often weigh these integrated platforms when choosing payment setups, as discussed in our analysis of Stripe vs Square options.

4. Embedded Insurance and Investments

Rather than purchasing insurance separately, consumers can add protection plans during checkout—such as extended warranties on electronics or flight protection during booking. Similarly, micro-investment tools let consumers automatically invest spare change from everyday purchases or allow freelancers to direct a portion of their earnings into retirement accounts directly inside payment apps.

Why are nonfinancial companies adopting embedded tools?

The shift toward embedded finance is heavily driven by changes in buyer expectations and business monetization goals. Integrating financial products into nonbank platforms offers several distinct advantages:

  • Reduced Checkout Friction: Storing payment methods or offering quick point-of-sale credit reduces cart abandonment rates.
  • New Revenue Streams: Nonfinancial companies can earn revenue through transaction fees, interchange splits, or interest-sharing arrangements with bank partners.
  • Higher Customer Retention: Operating branded deposit accounts or rewards cards keeps users engaged within a platform's software ecosystem longer.

Detailed analysis from TechTarget's embedded finance report highlights that 86% of mobile wallet users in the U.S. have completed purchases within a retailer's embedded mobile app. Furthermore, research from Juniper Research indicated that 65% of surveyed businesses that do not currently offer embedded financial tools plan to evaluate them. This adoption reflects broader structural shifts in software monetization detailed in our guide on fintech innovation trends.

While the digital execution is modern, the business logic builds on historical predecessors. Nonbank businesses have long offered branded private-label store credit cards or auto financing through car dealerships to secure customer loyalty. Modern APIs simply make those capabilities faster and accessible to online software providers.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does embedded finance differ from traditional banking?

Traditional banking requires customers to physically visit a branch or log into a bank's dedicated website to handle transactions. Embedded finance puts banking services directly inside nonfinancial software, allowing users to borrow, pay, or manage money at the moment they need it without leaving their current app.

What is the main benefit of embedded banking for businesses?

Embedded banking allows nonfinancial platforms to increase user retention and unlock new revenue streams. By providing branded accounts, cards, and instant payouts, software companies keep users inside their digital ecosystem while earning margins on card interchange and transaction fees.

How do nonbank platforms remain compliant with banking regulations?

Nonbank businesses partner with regulated sponsor banks and Banking-as-a-Service (BaaS) providers. The licensed financial institution handles compliance duties—including Know Your Customer (KYC) verification, Anti-Money Laundering (AML) monitoring, and deposit holding—while the platform delivers the user interface via APIs.

What is the difference between embedded payments and embedded lending?

Embedded payments focus on moving money directly from an existing payment source, such as saved credit cards or digital wallets. Embedded lending provides real-time access to credit—such as short-term BNPL installment loans or extended financing—at the point of sale.
